 The Interior Axial Fixed Point in the In-Plane
                  Three-Target Geometry:

Exact Existence, Location, and Its Independence
                        from Bandwidth

                                             Amit Alper

                                         Hebrew University of Jerusalem
                               Companion note to the bandwidth-Pareto series

                                       September 14, 2026

                                                     Abstract

    In the in-plane three-target geometry -- base targets a1,2 = (1, 0) and apex a3 = (0, e),
source on the symmetry axis x = 0 -- a population can fail to reach the apex even when
nothing pulls it sideways: an interior fixed point y  (0, e) of the purely axial dynamics
can trap it first. We derive exactly when this fixed point exists, where it sits, and how it
moves, as a function of the apex height e and the shape exponent . The central, somewhat
surprising fact is that the bandwidth  plays no role at all in this question: existence and
location of y depend only on (e, ). We prove: for  > 1 an interior fixedpoint exists for
every e > 0 and is unique; at the exact threshold  = 1 it exists iff e > 1/ 3 and, when it
exists, sits at the universal location y = 1/ 3 regardless of e; at the exactly solvable point
 = 2 it sits at y = e/3 for every e; and for  < 1 it appears only above a computable
threshold ec(), born in a saddle-node pair, with ec()  1/ 3 as   1-. Bandwidth's
role is confined entirely to a separate question -- whether small transverse perturbations
grow at that fixed point -- and we show exactly where the two questions cleanly decouple
and where they don't.

1 Setup

Fix the base half-spacing at b = 1 (as in the companion experiments) and place

               a1 = (-1, 0), a2 = (1, 0), a3 = (0, e), e > 0,

with a population approaching along the symmetry axis x = 0 from a source at (0, -h), h
large. On this axis, de(y) := 1 + y2 is the common distance to a1, a2 and d3(y) := |y - e| the
distance to a3. The exact axial velocity, from the bandwidth-Pareto flow, is

               y = F (y) := - 2y de(y)-2 + (y - e) d3(y)-2 .                    (1)

We ask: for which (e, ) does F have a zero y  (0, e) -- a genuine interior equilibrium, distinct
from the apex itself -- and how does y depend on the parameters?

6  < 1: a saddle-node bifurcation

For  < 1, both endpoints of (4) are negative, so the intermediate value theorem gives no
information. Direct computation shows that for small e, G < 0 throughout (0, e) (no interior
equilibrium: the population reaches the apex directly), while for large enough e, G develops a
local maximum that rises above zero, creating two roots -- one stable, one unstable.

Theorem 6.1 (Saddle-node threshold). For each  < 1 there is a threshold ec() such that:

  for e < ec(): no interior equilibrium exists;
  for e = ec(): exactly one, of saddle-node type (G(yc) = G(yc) = 0 simultaneously);
  for e > ec(): exactly two, ystable < yc < yunstable.

ec() is computed by eliminating y from G(y, e, ) = 0 and yG(y, e, ) = 0 (Section 9);
it decreases smoothly from ec  1.73 at   0+ to ec()  1/ 3 as   1-, matching
Proposition 5.2 exactly at the boundary.

   This was checked directly, not assumed

9 Numerical methods

All roots of G were found by sampling y  (10-4, e - 10-6) on a grid of 3­8 × 103 points and
applying bisection (scipy.optimize.brentq) at each sign change. The saddle-node curve was
traced by parametrizing candidate tangency points yc, solving G(yc, e, ) = 0 for e in closed form

                                                             6
(e = yc + [2yc(1 + yc2)(-2)/2]1/(-1)), and finding where yG along that curve changes sign. Axial
trajectories were validated by direct high-accuracy integration (Radau, rtol = 10-12) of (1),
matching every closed-form prediction in Sections 4­5 to better than 10-4. Full two-dimensional
trajectories used the exact N -cell bandwidth-Pareto flow with a tiny symmetry-breaking seed,
to test Proposition 8.1.

10 Conclusion

The interior axial fixed point that can trap a population short of the apex is entirely a fact
about the triangle's shape (e) and the kernel's sharpness () -- bandwidth  has no say in
whether it exists or where it sits. Three landmarks organize the whole picture:  = 1, where the
fixed point (if present) sits at the universal 1/ 3 and existence turns on at e = 1/ 3 exactly;
 = 2, where it sits at e/3 for every isosceles triangle; and the saddle-node curve for  < 1
that connects continuously to the  = 1 threshold. What  governs is a genuinely separate
question layered on top -- whether the population, once it would stop there, is stable against
sideways splitting -- and the two questions only decouple cleanly on the stable side of that
second threshold.

The central take-away for designing experiments: in the perpendicular geometry, |zc| is a single,
clean scalar that you can read directly off simulations (where does the fan open?) and that the
theory predicts without fitting parameters. Increasing  or  makes this number larger -- the
split happens further from the plane, earlier in the descent -- while e has almost no effect when
the source is placed at the centroid. This is the closest three-target analogue of the two-target
statement "higher  drives an earlier split." In the in-plane geometry the statement is more
nuanced:  moves the fixed point y but whether the population actually splits there depends
additionally on  -- the two questions cannot be fully separated.
